The HSA shoebox strategy, explained

By Parth Darji · Updated November 22, 2026

In shortPay medical bills with your own money, leave your HSA invested, keep every receipt, and withdraw the same amount tax-free whenever you choose. The IRS sets no deadline, but you must keep the records.

How it works

A Health Savings Account (HSA) lets you put money aside before tax if you have a qualifying high-deductible health plan (it's different from an FSA: HSA vs FSA). You can spend it on qualified medical expenses right away, but you don't have to.

The "shoebox" strategy uses one detail of the rules: there is no time limit for paying yourself back. So instead of swiping your HSA card at the pharmacy, you:

  1. Pay the medical bill yourself, from your checking account or a credit card.
  2. Keep the receipt, the old-fashioned way in a shoebox, or as a photo.
  3. Leave the HSA money invested so it can keep growing.
  4. Reimburse yourself later: withdraw the total of your saved receipts tax-free, next year or decades from now.

Why people do it

Under federal rules, money in an HSA can be tax-advantaged three times: contributions reduce your taxable income, growth inside the account isn't taxed, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free. The longer the money stays invested, the more of that tax-free growth you keep.

Your saved receipts also work like a tax-free emergency fund: if you ever need cash, you can withdraw up to the total of your unreimbursed receipts without tax.

An example

Say you pay a $500 dental bill yourself in 2026 and keep the receipt. If that $500 stays invested in your HSA and grows 7% a year, it could be about $1,935 after 20 years. You can then withdraw $500 tax-free for that old receipt, and the remaining growth stays in the account.

This is an illustration, not a promise: investments can lose value, and real returns vary. Try your own numbers with the HSA growth calculator.

The rules that matter

  • The expense must come after your HSA was opened. Bills from before the account existed don't qualify, even if you had a high-deductible plan.
  • It must be a qualified medical expense under IRS Publication 502. Not sure? Use the eligible expenses lookup.
  • No double dipping. You can't reimburse yourself for something insurance or an FSA already paid, or that you deducted on your tax return.
  • Keep records. If the IRS asks, you need to show the withdrawal paid for qualified expenses. See which receipts to keep.
  • Your family counts. Expenses for your spouse and tax dependents can qualify.

Risks and downsides

  • You need the cash now. Paying bills out of pocket only makes sense if it doesn't push you into debt.
  • Lost receipts mean lost tax-free withdrawals. Paper fades and gets thrown out; keep a copy you can find in 20 years.
  • Investments can fall. Leaving money invested is a choice with market risk.
  • Rules can change, and a few states (for example California and New Jersey) tax HSAs differently from the federal rules.
  • After age 65 you can withdraw for any reason without the 20% penalty, but non-medical withdrawals are taxed as income, so receipts still matter. Your HSA after 65.

How to start

  1. Check that your HSA provider lets you invest, and choose investments you're comfortable with.
  2. Next time you have a medical bill, pay it yourself and save an itemized receipt.
  3. Write down the date, provider, amount and what it was for, and keep track of what you haven't reimbursed yet.
  4. When you want the money, reimburse yourself and note the withdrawal date.

Questions

Is there a time limit to reimburse yourself from an HSA?

No. IRS Publication 969 sets no deadline, as long as the expense happened after you opened the HSA and you can show it was a qualified medical expense that wasn't paid another way.

Can I reimburse myself for expenses from before I opened my HSA?

No. Only expenses incurred after the HSA was established qualify.

Do I need to keep paper receipts?

No. The IRS accepts electronic copies of records that are accurate and readable, so a clear photo or PDF of an itemized receipt works.

What happens if I never reimburse myself?

Nothing bad. The money stays in your HSA. After 65 you can withdraw it for any reason, but non-medical withdrawals are taxed as income.
