Sole proprietor vs LLC taxes

By Parth Darji · Updated November 23, 2026

In shortFor federal tax, a one-person LLC is taxed exactly like a sole proprietor unless you choose otherwise. An LLC is mainly a legal choice (liability), not a tax saving. The tax change comes with an S corp election, which has its own costs.

Sole proprietor

You're one automatically when you work for yourself without forming a company. Business income and expenses go on Schedule C, and you pay self-employment tax on the profit plus income tax.

Single-member LLC

  • By default the IRS treats it as a "disregarded entity": same Schedule C, same self-employment tax, same estimated payments.
  • What changes: a separate legal entity that can help keep business debts apart from you, a state filing fee, and in some states yearly fees or taxes.
  • Keep a separate bank account either way. It makes records and deductions much easier.

LLC taxed as an S corporation

An LLC (or corporation) can elect S corp status. You then pay yourself a reasonable salary through payroll; Social Security and Medicare apply to that salary, and the remaining profit can come out as distributions without self-employment tax. In exchange you take on payroll, a separate business tax return and more bookkeeping costs. It tends to make sense only once profit is well above a reasonable salary for your work. Talk to a tax professional before electing.

Multi-member LLC

With two or more owners, an LLC is taxed as a partnership by default and files its own return (Form 1065), with each owner getting a Schedule K-1.

Either way

  • You still pay quarterly estimated taxes on profit as a sole proprietor or default LLC.
  • Your deductions are the same: see the freelancer deductions checklist.

Questions

Does an LLC lower my taxes?

Not by itself. A single-member LLC is taxed like a sole proprietor by default. Taxes change only if the LLC elects to be taxed as an S corporation or C corporation.

Does a single-member LLC pay self-employment tax?

Yes, by default. The profit goes on Schedule C and is subject to self-employment tax, just like a sole proprietor.

When is an S corp election worth it?

Usually only when profit is well above a reasonable salary for your work, since payroll and an extra tax return add cost. A tax professional can run the numbers for your case.
